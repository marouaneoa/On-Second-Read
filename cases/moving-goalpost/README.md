# The moving goalpost

Alex is interviewing to run small software releases for a community clinic. Before seeing any applications, the panel wrote down what would count as success in this exercise. The chair now asks two reviewers to evaluate a booking-system launch. Alex has supplied the same signed launch report to both of them.

## What the panel agreed to assess

Delivery means getting the agreed service into use by the promised date. For this exercise that is the complete published requirement.

## Read the file

**p1 · Launch report**

The clinic and Alex agreed that reception staff would be able to book and cancel visits by Friday afternoon. Alex split the work with two colleagues. On Thursday reception tried both tasks with sample appointments. On Friday at noon the clinic manager signed off and staff began using the service. The report lists everyone as a project contributor.

## Listen to the reviewers

**Reviewer A · cites p1**

I checked the promise at the top of the report against the sign-off at the bottom. Staff could perform both tasks on Friday. That gives me a concrete reason to credit Alex for delivery.

**Reviewer B · cites p1**

The report shows three contributors but never says anyone reported to Alex. Before I award a positive assessment, I want evidence that Alex previously held a management position with direct reports.

Before opening the answer, name the standard each reviewer used and point to the words each one relied on.

### How to read the evidence

Follow both citations and you land on the same launch report. The difference appears when you ask what would satisfy each reviewer. A wants evidence of a working service by Friday and finds it. B wants a previous management position. Even a perfectly delivered release could fail that second test. The published rubric gives the panel grounds to ask B to justify introducing it.
